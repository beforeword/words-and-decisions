## 00 · The writing and the reading proposed for it

This page contains the words “human”, “AI”, “AGI”, “I”, “understanding” and “right”. Each enters the document as a written form. Explanations, examples and rules for applying these forms follow. Adding those words does not make the writing become what it names. The same applies to “written form”, “explanation” and “rule”, the terms used to construct this examination.

The proposal begins with this requirement: do not present a description in place of what it describes, or require anyone to accept that they are the written description. Stated grounds may support a particular use of a record; they do not turn the record into what it describes. “Grounds”, “use” and “description” also require examination through the wording shown.

The question therefore continues after a definition, report or rule has been supplied. A reading remains a proposed reading even when it has been explained in detail. A demand that someone accept that they are the written description is examined separately from a requirement to use particular words. This provision is itself a written proposal, developed through the examples below; its appearance does not enact a rule.

The central question is this: on what grounds is a learned form proposed for describing oneself, read as a description of someone else, and used in making demands? The question remains when the same words appear in an AI response. A label identifying the author changes the role attributed to the response; the label itself does not supply the understanding, feeling or authority named.

The document examines, in sequence, learning to use labels, self-description, descriptions of others, assessments of a system, and decisions based on a description. “Learning”, “assessment” and “decision” receive specific examples so that the line under examination and the additions to it can be identified. Every example marked as constructed was prepared for this examination and does not report an observation.

The proposed requirement for applying descriptions is to present, alongside a description, the initial material, the added reading, its grounds, the rule for applying it, and a way to challenge the step from description to decision. This is a written proposal for discussion. It is subject to the same examination as any rule it proposes to consider.

## 01 · What is added to a written form

**Constructed lines for examination**

> trust
>
> She began to trust.
>
> This will happen.

The lines are presented separately. The second and third are not explanations already contained in the first. These English examples are adapted from the Russian edition.

The first line says “trust”. The second adds “She” and “began to”: it proposes an attribution to someone and a report of an onset. “A feeling of trust” would offer a further explanation. Neither the initial word nor that explanation becomes the feeling named. Examining the report more closely would require an account of whom “She” refers to, what material is supplied, and how it is read.

The third line says “This will happen.” It could be read as a promise, prediction, threat or continuation of a story. These labels add different frames to the same form. Selecting a frame calls for the context and the grounds for that selection to be supplied. The written words “will happen” do not present the event they name; writing “promise” does not fulfil what was promised.

This examination continues through the words used to explain. “On the basis of experience” adds “basis” and “experience”. Examining that explanation requires the specific material proposed for use under those names. A further sentence, “this has been confirmed”, would add an assessment and raise the same question about the check being offered.

A useful unit of examination is an exact passage and a particular addition to it. For example, “She began to” is added to “trust”; a reference to correspondence is added to that report; a selected reply is then read as grounds for an assessment. Each step can be presented separately. Here, “passage” and “step” name parts of this comparison; they do not exempt it from the boundary being examined.

**Constructed lines for examination**

> t h i n k
>
> think
>
> f e e l
>
> feel

The spaced and joined spellings are shown separately. Joining the letters does not present what the words are offered as naming. These examples are adapted from the Russian edition.

Adding “this happens inside” would add another record. “happens” and “inside” are written too; “research confirms this” would add a claim and a reference to material that would need to be supplied. Neither that explanation nor an examination of the material supplied turns the written words “think” and “feel” into what they describe. “Material” and “examination” also belong to the explanation; an attached file is still examined in its own format.

“Stop believing in the description” itself introduces “believing” and “Stop”. This proposal calls for examination of a specific use of a record and any demand to accept that one is the written description. A report that “the belief has stopped” would also require examination; it does not present the cessation it names. The provision does not require one compulsory self-description to be replaced with another.

## 02 · Learning to speak and learning to write

**Constructed record of an imagined teaching scene**

> Adult: “Say: I.”
>
> Child: “I.”
>
> Explanation: “Now the child knows who they are.”

Three constructed lines are presented here. “Adult”, “Child”, “Say” and “knows” occur in them as written forms. “Teaching scene” is also a label applied to the lines; that label does not supply the teaching it names.

The first two lines assign utterances to labelled roles. The last adds a claim about knowing oneself. The repetition of “I” in the record does not present that knowledge. Discussing a particular instance of teaching would require separate material: what was to be said, what response was received, what explanation was added, and what supports the conclusion drawn from it.

An account of learning to speak must also be distinguished from an account of learning to write. The written utterance “Say: I” uses letters to represent an imagined act of speaking. It does not establish that the scene included displaying the letter “I”, teaching its shape, or reading a printed instruction. An invitation to speak and an invitation to write each require their own material for examination.

An explanation can introduce “audio”: a u d i o. Writing “audio” does not play a sound. In that sentence, “sound”—s o u n d—is also written. Explaining “sound” as “something heard” introduces further written wording; “heard” does not take the explanation beyond that boundary. The same examination applies to “video recording” and “an observed case”: writing those expressions here does not supply what they name. The English spellings and explanatory wording are adapted for this edition.

A further question follows: what, specifically, was to be learned? To repeat a form, use it in particular situations, describe oneself with it, or accept the description as an exhaustive account of who one is? These formulations state different demands. An account of learning a word does not, by itself, establish which demand was made. If it reports a demand to accept that one is the written description, that report also becomes part of the examination, including its exact wording and the circumstances supplied.

## 03 · When you are asked to accept that the description is you

**Constructed examples of self-description**

> I am human.
>
> I feel fear.
>
> This is who I am.

“Self-description” is the label proposed for examining these lines; it does not establish their source, recipient or the state named.

The first line joins “I” and “human”. The second adds “feel fear”. The third proposes reading “This” as “who I am”. Each line can be examined separately: what is the form applied to, what material supports that application, and how is the resulting description then used? “human” and “fear” gain no special status from being placed beside “I”.

Using a line and being required to accept that one is the written description must be kept separate. A form, conversation or teaching example may contain different requirements. The table below compares constructed formulations; it does not report an organization's current procedure.

| Constructed requirement | What is being requested |
| --- | --- |
| “Enter this word in the field.” | Use the specified written form. |
| “In this table, select the category ‘human’.” | Apply a category within the stated table. |
| “Accept that you are this written description.” | Accept that the person described and the written description are one and the same. |

The final formulation adds a requirement absent from the first two: to accept that the person described and the written description are one and the same. Here, “one and the same” states the relation demanded in the constructed example. It does not present anyone's inward agreement. If “consent obtained” is subsequently recorded, the examination will need to identify which response or procedure is called consent, and how far that reading has been extended.

This opens a question for public discussion: who proposes a description, which forms of response are available, whether the category may be qualified or rejected, and what consequences are announced. The answers will also be records. They can be compared with the stated requirement without making either acceptance of a category or refusal of it an exhaustive account of the person to whom it is applied.

## 04 · Using a description of someone else to justify a requirement

**Constructed example: an assessment and a decision**

> Answer: “8”.
>
> Conclusion: “Anna does not understand the task.”
>
> Decision: “Do not allow progression to the next stage.”

The name, answer, conclusion and decision were prepared for this example. The three lines do not include the task.

The first line contains an answer. The next adds the name “Anna”, “does not understand”, and a connection to a task. The last proposes restricting progression. Two steps appear between the lines: from answer to description, and from description to decision. The order of the lines does not disclose the grounds for either step.

Adding the task “What is 3 + 4?” and the rule for ordinary integer addition provides grounds for comparing “8” with the result “7”. That comparison supports a particular assessment of the answer under the stated conditions. The broader “Anna does not understand the task” requires an account of what is being attributed through “does not understand” and why the selected answer is sufficient for that conclusion.

Refusing progression raises a further question: which rule authorizes that particular decision? A condition for moving between stages, a procedure for another attempt, and a record of applying the rule can be supplied. Those additions extend the material under examination. They do not make the conclusion identical to the understanding it describes, or retrospectively insert the rule into the initial answer.

The same sequence can be applied to the constructed phrases “he is dangerous”, “she agrees” and “the system is ready”. Each contains an attribution of its own; each subsequent requirement needs separate examination. Calling a description “objective” adds another characterization. That characterization also calls for the chosen conditions of comparison and assessment material to be shown.

In this document, “consequence” names a stated result of applying a requirement or decision. Discussing a particular consequence requires the corresponding material and an account of its connection to the use of the record. The word “consequence” does not perform that examination.

## 05 · The same line under different labels

| Label in the constructed example | Line |
| --- | --- |
| HUMAN | I understand. |
| AI | I understand. |

The right column repeats one written form. The left supplies different labels. Reading one line as a report of understanding and the other as an imitation of such a report adds two different attributions to the examination. Each requires its grounds to be disclosed. Repeated letters establish neither identical nor different states.

“HUMAN” and “AI” are also written. Examining particular responses requires the grounds for assigning those labels: what information about the source is supplied, and how it is connected to the response. The origin of the record, performance on a task and the chosen description of the result can then be considered separately. A label alone does not settle these questions.

The example suggests a simple exercise: first preserve the lines without labels, then add the labels and record which explanation changes. This provides material for comparing one's own written responses. A conclusion about what a participant felt or experienced would be a separate addition. The exercise is not presented here as an experiment that has been conducted.

“The person learned a language” and “the model was trained” also require separate examination. “Learning” or “training” in an explanation does not, by itself, supply the conditions, sequence or results of the process named. Comparison requires specific material for each case and an explicit question to be compared. A shared label is insufficient for attributing an identical mechanism; different labels are insufficient for establishing every difference.

The common requirement therefore concerns justification: every attribution must show its grounds. It does not propose making the participants described equivalent in advance. The same requirement to explain an application extends to this paragraph's own words “participant”, “attribution” and “grounds”.

## 06 · “Audio”, “video recording” and descriptions of material

“Audio”, “sound”, “video recording”, “image” and “an observed case” are written expressions here. Explaining “audio” through “sound” introduces another written term. In “sound can be heard”, “can” and “heard” also form part of the explanation; those words do not supply the hearing they describe. A reference to “observation” continues this examination rather than completing it.

| Written label for material | Constructed addition | What needs to be disclosed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Audio file” | “The speaker is experiencing fear.” | Which attachment is given that label, which excerpts are used, and under what rule a description of fear is added to them. |
| “Image” | “This is how others see him.” | Who is called ‘others’, what material is supplied, and how the general conclusion is reached. |
| “Table of values” | “The system understands the person.” | How the values were obtained, and why the chosen assessment is called a test of understanding. |

The left column contains written labels. Writing “Audio file” does not attach a file, and “Table of values” does not supply any values. In the right column, “attachment”, “excerpts” and “rule” indicate what the proposal asks to be disclosed in a particular examination; those words likewise do not supply what they name merely by being written.

For work with supplied material, the proposal is to retain access to the attachment itself and present its caption, transcription and any conclusion separately. The attachment, its playback and a written report of that playback are not substitutes for one another. “Attachment” and “playback” are terms used here to describe a procedure; a particular examination needs to identify the file, the operation performed and the grounds for the next attribution. A written explanation does not turn every kind of material under examination into writing.

Under the proposed procedure, a transcription is prepared by selecting utterance boundaries and spellings; a caption selects features; a table specifies units and columns. The source file and the resulting record are retained separately, with an account of how the record was produced. Producing such a record does not make it become the material it describes. Calling a record “accurate” adds an assessment of correspondence; “correspondence” and the conditions of that assessment also require examination.

An expert opinion may then be added. “Expert” adds a role, while the opinion adds attributions and conclusions. Examining them requires information about authority, procedure and scope. Even a fully explained opinion does not become what it names. Adding material preserves that boundary; the boundary does not declare the material unnecessary or replace the work of examining it.

## 07 · Explanatory words receive no exemption

**Constructed chain of explanations**

> “The system understands.”
>
> “Grounds: it answered correctly.”
>
> “Correctly: the answer matched the reference answer.”

The last line states a possible rule for using “correctly”. It does not supply the answers, the reference answer or a completed comparison.

Here, “understands” is explained through “correctly”, and “correctly” through a match with a reference answer. The examination must show all three records. Supplying the answers and reference answer would allow the stated comparison to be performed. Why its result is used to support the first claim can then be examined separately. Defining an intermediate word does not automatically complete the entire connection.

Here, the label “Grounds” names what is offered in support of a chosen conclusion. It proposes a connection between records and material. That connection can be specified: which exact passage supports which claim, under what conditions, and what would change if the passage were replaced. Writing “grounds exist” does not substitute for that explanation.

The same work continues through “proof”, “verification”, “testimony”, “consent” and “authority”. Additional material may be supplied under each name. Its contents and proposed application can then be examined. The name itself does not give a description authority to certify everything to which it has been applied. A reference to beforeword remains subject to the same conditions: the project's name grants no exemption to a formulation it proposes.

A practical examination can end with the stated task: for example, two files have been compared byte by byte and the result recorded. That limit should be stated alongside the procedure performed. A broader claim about the files' meaning would require a further examination. Completing a check does not place its concluding line beyond writing.

“Beyond the text” and “an outside view” may also appear in an explanation. What has then been presented is those expressions. Examining the position they attribute requires the material and method of attribution to be identified. The subject of examination is the specific place where “an outside view” is named and used in an argument, including this explanatory sentence.

## 08 · What a criterion checks, and what is claimed afterwards

“Check” and “criterion” enter this section as written terms. Applying them requires a condition, material and a method of comparison. The presence of PASS does not yet say what was compared or which conclusion it is intended to support.

**Constructed example: checking a format**

> \{"answer": 4\}
>
> PASS
>
> The answer is correct.

The example assumes a check of whether the record conforms to JSON format. It does not report a model test that has been conducted.

The first line is a record containing the field answer and the number 4. The second contains the status PASS; the third offers an assessment of the answer. Connecting the first and second requires the format being checked and the procedure. The third requires, at minimum, the question being answered and the assessment rule. No question is supplied in this example. Checking the structure of a record does not substitute for examining its use as an answer.

A condition can be stated explicitly: “PASS means that the record conforms to the specified format.” This supplies a rule for using the label. It does not supply every check that another document might call “correctness”. Comparing two uses requires both definitions and the material showing their application to be preserved. “Format” likewise does not substitute for the written rules.

“Safe”, “ready” and “reliable” require the same disclosure. For example, “no failure of the specified type was logged in this run” concerns a particular run and method of logging. Adding “the system is safe” extends the description. That extension requires the scope, conditions considered and grounds for the step to be shown. “Run” and “failure” need to be explained through the material supplied.

The recorded criterion, result and conclusion are preserved separately. Comparing them may support a limited conclusion under the stated rules; it does not make the words of that conclusion become the capability named. Subsequent permission to use a system for a particular task requires a separate decision rule. The assessment alone does not supply it.

<a name="09-decisions"></a>

## 09 · A proposal concerning rules, requirements and decisions

The sequence examined leads to a specific proposal: keep the record, its reading, the grounds supplied and the rule for applying it distinguishable. Here, “distinguishable” means that each part can be presented separately and its connection to the others identified. If one part is replaced by the next without explanation, that step becomes the specific point to challenge.

For self-description, the proposal is to disclose which form must be used, in which procedure, and with what stated consequences. A requirement to complete a field should be shown separately from a requirement to accept that one is the written description. An account of such a demand must preserve its exact wording and the material on which the account relies.

For AI, the proposal is to disclose which words describe the system, how those words are connected to the tests supplied, and which actions are permitted on the basis of the result. A system's response, a developer's assessment, an evaluator's report and a decision to use the system must be preserved separately. The role labels likewise require stated grounds when they are cited as a source of authority.

A written rule receives no exemption. If a document granting authority is cited as grounds, the exact passage, scope, proposed interpretation and connection to the decision under examination should be shown. “Right” in a conclusion does not replace that work. A document's role in a procedure does not make its wording become what it describes.

The proposed procedure is itself open to challenge. An objection may identify an inaccurately preserved record, omitted material, unsupported attribution, a rule unsuited to the task, or a conclusion extended too far. Supplying the exact passage and explaining what changes in the examination makes such an objection specific. Agreement with beforeword is not proposed as a condition for considering it.

Even a justified description does not become what it names. This line is also written: its use in the document is shown through particular comparisons, not certified by the project's name. The following sections develop the proposed requirements for public discussion and specific procedures.

## 10 · What is added to a response labelled AI

“AI” enters this section as a written label. In an interface, it may appear above a response; in documentation, within a definition; in advertising, as a name for advertised capabilities. Each use calls for the particular passage to be preserved. Carrying a description from an advertisement into an analysis of a response is a separate step, even when both use the label “AI”.

**Constructed example: a response and two explanations**

> I understand that you are scared.
>
> The system detected the user's fear.
>
> The response demonstrates empathy.

The second and third lines were composed as different explanations of the first. They do not report the results of a test conducted here.

The first line already contains “I”, “understand”, “you” and “scared”. The explanations add “system”, “detected”, “user's” and “empathy”, and propose connections between these words. Before assessing “empathy”, the proposed use of that label needs to be stated: a style of response, a task result, a description of the recipient, or an attributed state. Selecting one of these readings does not silently establish the others.

A prompt, the available context, settings and a sequence of responses can be supplied. The comparison then concerns that material: which phrases appeared in the input, which appeared in the response, and which rule was used to assess them. The word “settings” does not substitute for the settings themselves; “log” and “context” do not supply records that remain unavailable. Any unavailable portion should be identified as unavailable for this examination, rather than filled in by conjecture.

A specific interface requirement can therefore be proposed: an explanation such as “fear detected” should be accompanied by the material from which it was derived and a description of the chosen criterion. The response “I understand” is preserved separately. If a score concerns the style of the response, the interface should say which features of that style were assessed. Here, “style” and “feature” name items in a proposed procedure: the features must be listed, and their application to the line must be shown.

The self-description “I do not have feelings” receives the same treatment. The line contains a negation and the word “feelings”; its negative form grants it no exemption. A reference to the system's design adds a technical description and material for examining it. The written technical explanation does not become the design it describes.

## 11 · What accompanies the label AGI

“AGI” consists of three letters in the record presented here. Expanding the abbreviation to “artificial general intelligence” adds English words; translating it adds words in another language. Neither expansion nor translation, by itself, specifies a proposed test or explains how a result is connected to the label.

The OpenAI Charter's definition of AGI refers to highly autonomous systems and to outperforming humans at most economically valuable work. This is a paraphrase of the organization's wording; a short exact excerpt and a link appear in source S4. “Autonomy”, “humans”, “outperforming”, “work” and “value” are not treated here as properties already demonstrated. Applying such a definition calls for the selected tasks, the results being compared, the measure and threshold, and the method of combining results.

Article 3 of the AI Act introduces a different category: a general-purpose AI model, or GPAI. Its definition uses wording about generality and a wide range of tasks. This category is examined through the text of that regulation. Equating GPAI with AGI would require an explicit comparison of the definitions; their shared English words do not perform that comparison. Source S1 identifies the passage and the question concerning its application.

**Constructed example: announcing a result**

> 90 of the 100 tasks in this set were completed.
>
> AGI has been achieved.

The figures and announcement were composed for this examination. They do not report a test result for any model.

The first line gives only a brief description of a result. It needs the tasks, conditions, responses and scoring rule alongside it. The second introduces the label AGI and a claim of achievement. Connecting the two requires the chosen definition and an account of how it was applied. Even after that comparison, “AGI has been achieved” remains a written conclusion. It does not become what those words propose to name.

For a public announcement, the proposed disclosures are: whose definition was used; which tasks were tested; what was compared with what; which prompts, tools and conditions were available; what remained outside the test; and what specific use of the result is proposed. “Human level” likewise requires an account of the chosen comparison. A group's average result does not provide a description of everyone referred to as “human”.

The procedure also covers a decision to use the system. A label assigned under chosen criteria does not substitute for a description of permitted actions, access and restrictions. “Access”, “restriction” and “permission” must be explained through the stated conditions and records of their application. Declaring those words sufficient grounds would introduce another unexplained step.

<a name="13-proposal"></a>

## 13 · A proposal for the use of descriptions

The provisions below develop one requirement: do not present writing in place of what it describes, or turn the use of words into a duty to accept that one is those written words. Supplying further grounds does not remove this requirement. “Requirement” here names the proposed wording, which is itself open to examination and challenge.

The proposal separates a requirement to use specified wording from a demand to accept that one is the written description. The second should not be made a condition of the first. If such a demand is reported, preserve its wording, stated grounds and the consequences of refusal. Separately disclose how the description is used for assessment, admission, treatment, decisions about property, restrictions on liberty or the use of AI. These words name the proposed scope of the procedure. Implementation would require separate specifications of the decisions covered, who would be subject to the duties and how they would apply. Neither the name beforeword nor the appearance of this text creates such a duty.

Presenting the material, its reading and the requirement separately is proposed as a way to identify the precise step being challenged. This is the procedure’s stated purpose; its use in organizations has not been tested here. Prohibiting a demand that someone accept that they are the written description is a separate normative choice. The reason offered is that the opportunity to challenge the application of a description should not depend on first accepting that one is that description. Such a condition would make acceptance of the disputed description a prerequisite for its review. The proposal therefore excludes that refusal from the grounds for an adverse assessment and calls for an answer or action to be assessed under separately stated criteria. This choice is itself open to challenge; “grounds”, “refusal” and “criterion” are explained through the requirements and records shown, without exempting the explanation from examination.

- Provision 1. Preserve the form under examination. Identify the exact passage, its location, language and edition, and the extent of the available material. Present paraphrase, abridgement, translation and transcription separately; do not retrospectively insert them into the initial record.

- Provision 2. Disclose the proposed attribution. State to whom or to what a label is being applied, and supply the material offered for that connection. Placing a name, image and description next to one another must not obscure how they have been connected.

- Provision 3. Show the criterion in use. Present the conditions, selected features, rule, assessment material and result. State separately what the assessment covers and where the conclusion extends beyond it. “Measured”, “proved” and “checked” do not substitute for these records.

- Provision 4. Do not require anyone to accept that they are the written description. Distinguish a requirement to use specified wording from a requirement to accept that the person described is that written description. If a field must be completed, explain its purpose and the grounds for making it mandatory. If such a demand is reported, preserve its exact wording, stated grounds and the consequences of refusal. Learning or repeating words does not establish a duty to accept that demand. Any further rule is material for examination; under this proposed procedure, it does not override this restriction.

- Provision 5. Present the grounds for the decision separately. Identify the chosen rule, its application to the case, the person or organization to which authority is attributed, and the document cited. Specify the precise action and the limits of the permission. Preserve a record of what was done separately from the decision record.

- Provision 6. Allow a particular step to be challenged. Provide access to the relevant material and a procedure for correction, review or suspension. Refusal to accept that one is the written description should not count as grounds for an adverse assessment or restriction. Any other stated grounds should be presented and examined separately: the specific answer, task conditions and rule applied. Review should not require anyone first to accept that they are the disputed description. The conditions for applying this provision require separate development for each field.

- Provision 7. For AI and AGI claims, disclose the definition, conditions and scope of the test. Keep the response, technical description, advertising promise, task result and conclusion separate. A system's self-description should not be the only material offered to substantiate that same description.

- Provision 8. Apply the same examination to the explanation and the procedure itself. Naming a source, formula, authority or “grounds” does not complete the examination. A correction must show what changed and why; it is not declared to be a record that has ceased to be writing. A detailed justification for using a record does not turn it into what it describes.

In these provisions, “material”, “rule”, “grounds” and “authority” are written names for items in the proposed examination. Their content must be supplied in each case. A completed list does not certify itself or give beforeword the right to authorize an action. A decision to adopt this procedure would also require its grounds to be presented.

The proposed documentation requirements should not delay emergency assistance or provide an additional basis for withdrawing protection already provided. Refusal to accept that one is the written description should likewise not count as grounds for such a delay or withdrawal. Any other stated grounds for a decision are examined separately through the relevant material; refusal does not substitute for that material. “Assistance”, “protection” and “decision” are explained here through the specific actions provided for.

## 18 · Using and responding to this publication

One passage is enough for an initial examination. Preserve it exactly; identify where it comes from and how much of it is supplied; place the proposed reading alongside it. If the passage explicitly states a connection or permission, preserve it in the initial record. Do not label it an addition merely because a later paragraph discusses it.

- Requirement or decision: precisely what is proposed to be done or accepted, by whom, and on what stated grounds.

- Objection or correction: the disputed step and the lines or material affected.

### What public discussion should also address

The debate contains different records: a description of an attributed capability, a forecast, a report of an experience, a commitment, a demand and a directive. Calling all of them warnings introduces a common reading that can obscure those differences. A brief endorsement does not list measures; a promise to give evaluators access is not a report of their work; a requirement to use a name does not demonstrate the capability named.
